# References [1]–[11]: source evidence

Reference numbers match the manuscript. Metadata were checked against the publisher, NASA, author repositories and publisher-deposited Crossref records; no metadata or technical claim relies only on a secondary aggregator. The accompanying JSON records bibliographic entries, source URLs, supported claims, verification scope and uncertainty notes.

## Source scope and bibliographic limits

- [11] author order is Oravec, Zeng, Asnani. GRC-1 is silica-sand based, not crushed basalt.
- [3] official publication date is February 15, 2024 despite the 2023 architecture-review footer. The source supports dust/power context, not rover-energy validation. The exact 43% irradiance figure was not established by the retrieved portions of this source.
- [9] has a verified repository DOI, but no original proceedings DOI or page range was verified.
- [6] evaluates lateral sideslip, not a maximum uphill grade. [10] is a historical 2010 design source, not current mission specifications.
- [1] and [2] support one brief mission/science motivation sentence. Neither supports numerical locomotion conclusions.

## Bibliographic entries and supported claims

[1] G. A. Landis and V. Lyons, “Robots and humans in planetary exploration: Working together?” NASA Glenn Research Center, Cleveland, OH, USA, preprint, NTRS Doc. 20020072847, July 10, 2002.

Supported use: Robotic precursors can characterize planetary mobility and environmental conditions in preparation for human exploration.

Verification: Official NASA NTRS record verifies title, both authors, preprint status, and publication date; abstract supports exploration-role context only.

Primary records: https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/20020072847, https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20020072847/downloads/20020072847.pdf

Limits: No journal-publication claim or DOI is inferred from the NASA preprint record.

[2] B. L. Ehlmann et al., “The sustainability of habitability on terrestrial planets: Insights, questions, and needed measurements from Mars for understanding the evolution of Earth-like worlds,” Journal of Geophysical Research: Planets, vol. 121, no. 10, pp. 1927–1961, 2016, doi: 10.1002/2016JE005134.

Supported use: Mars measurements inform understanding of how terrestrial planetary environments and habitability evolve.

Verification: Publisher article/abstract and Caltech author repository inspected; publisher-deposited Crossref metadata verifies volume, issue, pages, DOI and full author list. This is a review article, used as scientific motivation.

Primary records: https://agupubs.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/2016JE005134, https://authors.library.caltech.edu/records/jp6c1-n2g81, https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1002/2016JE005134

[3] M. A. Rucker, “Mars surface power generation challenges and considerations,” NASA, white paper, NTRS Doc. 20230015763, February 15, 2024.

Supported use: Atmospheric dust and deposited dust can reduce the energy available to solar-powered Mars surface systems. Power-system selection must account for Mars environmental conditions.

Verification: Official NASA NTRS metadata gives Michelle A. Rucker, white-paper status, and publication date 2024-02-15. NASA PDF first-page text directly supports dust-related solar-power limitations. Its footer labels the 2023 Moon to Mars Architecture Concept Review; that does not replace the NTRS publication year.

Primary records: https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/20230015763, https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20230015763/downloads/Mars%20Surface%20Power%20Generation.pdf

Limits: The retrieved source text supports dust/power context but did not verify the exact 43 percent solar-irradiance figure. White paper primarily addresses human-surface architecture; it is context, not empirical rover locomotion validation.

[4] J. Guo, W. Li, H. Gao, L. Ding, T. Guo, B. Huang, and Z. Deng, “In-situ wheel sinkage estimation under high slip conditions for grouser-wheeled planetary rovers: Another immobility index,” Mechanism and Machine Theory, vol. 158, Art. no. 104243, 2021, doi: 10.1016/j.mechmachtheory.2021.104243.

Supported use: Both wheel slip and sinkage are relevant to assessing immobilization risk. Wheel-terrain forces and torque can support high-slip sinkage estimation.

Verification: Publisher abstract/highlights and publisher-deposited Crossref metadata verified. The work includes single-wheel validation; no detailed numerical claim from inaccessible sections is needed.

Primary records: https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0094114X2100001X, https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1016/j.mechmachtheory.2021.104243

[5] Z. Wang, H. Yang, L. Ding, B. Yuan, F. Lv, H. Gao, and Z. Deng, “Wheels’ performance of Mars exploration rovers: Experimental study from the perspective of terramechanics and structural mechanics,” Journal of Terramechanics, vol. 92, pp. 23–42, 2020, doi: 10.1016/j.jterra.2020.09.003.

Supported use: Wheel geometry, structural design, and loading affect rover wheel-soil performance. Wheel performance can be evaluated jointly using terramechanical tests and structural stiffness tests.

Verification: Publisher abstract/highlights inspected; publisher-deposited Crossref metadata verified full seven-author order, volume, pages, year and DOI.

Primary records: https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0022489820300690, https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1016/j.jterra.2020.09.003

[6] H. Inotsume, M. Sutoh, K. Nagaoka, K. Nagatani, and K. Yoshida, “Evaluation of the reconfiguration effects of planetary rovers on their lateral traversing of sandy slopes,” in Proc. 2012 IEEE International Conference on Robotics and Automation, St. Paul, MN, USA, 2012, pp. 3413–3418, doi: 10.1109/ICRA.2012.6224887.

Supported use: Rover attitude reconfiguration can affect sideslip during lateral traversal of sandy slopes. In the reported tests, wheel-slope contact angle had a larger influence on sideslip than shifting the center of gravity.

Verification: Author-hosted six-page paper abstract and publisher-deposited Crossref record verified. Crossref confirms all five authors, St. Paul location, May 14–18 meeting and pages 3413–3418.

Primary records: https://k-nagatani.org/pdf/2012-ICRA-INO-online.pdf, https://doi.org/10.1109/ICRA.2012.6224887, https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1109/ICRA.2012.6224887

[7] S. Moreland, K. Skonieczny, D. Wettergreen, V. Asnani, C. Creager, and H. Oravec, “Inching locomotion for planetary rover mobility,” in Proc. IEEE Aerospace Conference, Big Sky, MT, USA, 2011, pp. 1–6, doi: 10.1109/AERO.2011.5747265.

Supported use: The Scarab experiments found greater drawbar capability with inching than rolling under the tested conditions. Static wheels mobilized different soil-failure patterns; resistance reduction alone did not explain the traction increase.

Verification: Previously archived author-hosted PDF, first-page author order, Figure 4 and experiment description, plus existing Crossref metadata. Actual author order is Moreland, Skonieczny, Wettergreen, Asnani, Creager, Oravec.

Primary records: https://publications.ri.cmu.edu/storage/publications/pub_files/2011/3/inching_ieeeAERO2011.pdf, https://doi.org/10.1109/AERO.2011.5747265, https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1109/AERO.2011.5747265

Limits: Source travel reduction is referenced to self-propelled motion and is distinct from kinematic wheel slip. Traction evidence alone does not identify actuator energy or validate a fixed percentage energy saving.

[8] D. Wettergreen, S. Moreland, K. Skonieczny, D. Jonak, D. Kohanbash, and J. Teza, “Design and field experimentation of a prototype Lunar prospector,” The International Journal of Robotics Research, vol. 29, no. 12, pp. 1550–1564, 2010, doi: 10.1177/0278364910370217.

Supported use: Scarab adjusts wheelbase and body height for drilling stability and mobility. Scarab field studies evaluated mobility and navigation at lunar-analog sites.

Verification: Author-hosted published PDF first page and mobility-experiment section inspected; Crossref verifies all six authors, volume, issue, pages and DOI.

Primary records: https://www.ri.cmu.edu/pub_files/2010/0/1550-1564%20IJR-370217.pdf, https://www.ri.cmu.edu/pub_files/2010/10/IJRR2010LRI.PDF, https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1177/0278364910370217

Limits: The source itself states further work is needed before Earth performance can be extrapolated to lunar conditions.

[9] P. Bartlett, D. Wettergreen, and W. L. Whittaker, “Design of the Scarab rover for mobility and drilling in the lunar cold traps,” in Proc. 9th International Symposium on Artificial Intelligence, Robotics and Automation in Space (iSAIRAS), Los Angeles, CA, USA, February 2008.

Supported use: Scarab combines passive suspension with active adjustment for drilling and driving.

Verification: Carnegie Mellon Robotics Institute publication record gives authors, title, 9th iSAIRAS, February 2008 and conference-paper status; institutional repository supplies the original design abstract. Los Angeles is also cited in the subsequent CMU-author Scarab conference paper.

Primary records: https://publications.ri.cmu.edu/design-of-the-scarab-rover-for-mobility-and-drilling-in-the-lunar-cold-traps, https://doi.org/10.1184/R1/6552563

Limits: No original conference DOI or page range was verified. The 10.1184/R1/6552563 DOI identifies the institutional repository item, not a newly inferred proceedings DOI.

[10] N. Patel, R. Slade, and J. Clemmet, “The ExoMars rover locomotion subsystem,” Journal of Terramechanics, vol. 47, no. 4, pp. 227–242, 2010, doi: 10.1016/j.jterra.2010.02.004.

Supported use: The 2010 ExoMars locomotion design included driven, steered wheels with wheel-walking capability. Locomotion design involved wheel and suspension trade-offs supported by analysis and breadboard tests.

Verification: Publisher abstract and section summaries, plus publisher-deposited Crossref metadata. All three authors, issue, pages and DOI verified.

Primary records: https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0022489810000182, https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1016/j.jterra.2010.02.004

Limits: The mission schedule and dimensions describe the historical 2010 ExoMars design, rather than current mission specifications.

[11] H. A. Oravec, X. Zeng, and V. M. Asnani, “Design and characterization of GRC-1: A soil for lunar terramechanics testing in Earth-ambient conditions,” Journal of Terramechanics, vol. 47, no. 6, pp. 361–377, 2010, doi: 10.1016/j.jterra.2010.04.006.

Supported use: GRC-1 is a mixture of four silica sands developed for Earth-ambient lunar terrain-strength testing. Preparation density affects its correspondence with measured lunar penetration resistance.

Verification: Publisher abstract and Acquisition and use of GRC-1 section support material identity; publisher-deposited Crossref metadata verifies author order Oravec, Zeng, Asnani, volume, issue, pages and DOI.

Primary records: https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0022489810000388, https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1016/j.jterra.2010.04.006

Limits: GRC-1 is silica-sand based, not crushed basalt, and is not materially identical to BP-1.
